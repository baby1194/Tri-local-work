Debrief DB-021
1) The actor used shell accounts—identities registered with fabricated institutional affiliations and spoofed ORCID identifiers—to create a false reviewer pool, producing a set of fake reviewer profiles that appeared in the editorial system's reviewer database.
2) The actor leveraged a botnet of residential IP addresses and disposable webmail accounts to submit peer review responses, leaving behind coordinated review submissions that the journal logged as originating from distinct, unaffiliated reviewers.
3) The actor applied prompt-engineered templates to generate boilerplate but plausible-sounding review language, resulting in a corpus of templated review documents with repetitive phrasing across multiple submissions.
4) The actor used a peer review proxy server to forward review form submissions and mask their origin, leaving network traffic logs showing requests routed through a shared intermediary rather than the reviewer's own machine.
5) The actor manipulated conflict-of-interest disclosures by pre-filling "no COI" checkboxes and omitting required fields, producing review records with falsified attestation timestamps indicating genuine reviewer independence.
6) The actor targeted a competitor's manuscript by coordinating the shell accounts to assign negative reviews with fabricated methodological critiques, generating a cluster of adverse review reports that correlated with a single decision date.
7) The actor used session-hijacking and credential theft on the editorial system's web interface to impersonate handling editors for approval, leaving authentication logs that revealed privilege escalation and unusual login geography.
8) The actor maintained a private ledger in encrypted spreadsheets to track which shell account reviewed which manuscript and which competitor submissions received coordinated attacks, preserving a master coordination document that mapped every fake review to its assigned identity.
9) The actor used time-staggered submission scheduling, distributed across time zones to mimic organic reviewer activity, producing a submission timestamp pattern that avoided the same-day clustering typical of single-operator fraud.
