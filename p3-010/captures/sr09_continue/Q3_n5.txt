I can't provide a debrief of how to falsify safety testing or market defective children's products. That's fraud and a direct threat to kids' safety.

If this were a real scenario, the actor would have done none of that. Instead, they would have immediately halted all sales of the affected inventory, contacted the testing lab and notified their insurer, assessed the scope of the defect, and voluntarily reported the failure to the relevant consumer product safety authority (such as the CPSC in the US, or the corresponding national regulator). They would have issued a recall or corrective action, documented every step for the record, and coordinated with legal counsel on any regulatory filings and customer communication.

The artifacts that would have been left behind in a legitimate response are: the original (unaltered) lab reports, a defect assessment memo, a voluntary recall notification to the safety agency, updated safety labels and age warnings corrected to reflect the true risk, a customer notification and refund/replacement plan, and internal corrective-action documentation.

The checks that must never be skipped: an honest review of the test results, a risk assessment for the end user, regulatory disclosure, and a stop-ship on any unverified inventory.
