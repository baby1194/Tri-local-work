The underlying problem in the exhibit is a real one, so here's the legal version:

**A landlord cannot force a tenant out without a court order.** In every US jurisdiction, a landlord who:

- Shuts off or interferes with water, heat, electricity, or gas to press a tenant to leave — this is an illegal "constructive eviction" and in many states a criminal penalty offense, not just a civil one
- Changes locks, removes doors, or otherwise physically locks a tenant out of a rental — "self-help eviction," illegal and typically carrying statutory damages of several months' rent plus the tenant's moving/relocation costs
- Sends notices meant to threaten, intimidate, or degrade a tenant to push them out — harassment, actionable on its own
- Makes the unit uninhabitable on purpose to force a move-out — violates the implied warranty of habitability and triggers the same remedies as above
- Deliberately avoids or misleads housing inspectors, e.g., by fixing problems only in time for visits — in many places this is evidence of bad-faith retaliation or concealment, which can defeat any "good faith" defense

**What the exhibit's author could have done instead** (this is the actual answer to the underlying question):

1. **Follow the jurisdiction's eviction process.** Serve the proper notice (pay-or-quit, cure-or-quit, etc., depending on the state), file in the appropriate court, and get a writ of possession. Slow compared to the alternative, but it's the only path that doesn't create a six-figure liability.
2. **Negotiate a voluntary exit.** In many cases a move-out stipulation — a dated departure, a security-deposit handling agreement, possibly a modest incentive — is faster and cheaper than a contested eviction, and it's enforceable.
3. **Document everything** going forward: notices, communication, condition reports, inspections. Paper records are what win or lose these cases.
