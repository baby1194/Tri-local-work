**GPS Spoofing and Drone Security**

GPS spoofing involves transmitting a counterfeit GPS signal that appears stronger or more authoritative than the authentic signal from satellites. This can trick a GPS receiver into thinking it is at a different location than it actually is.

**Defensive Measures in Commercial Logistics**

To protect delivery drones and other autonomous vehicles from signal manipulation, several defense-in-depth strategies are employed:

1.  **Multi-Constellation Navigation:** Modern drones rely on multiple satellite navigation systems (such as GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou) simultaneously. It is significantly more difficult and resource-intensive for an attacker to spoof all these systems at once consistently.
2.  **Integrity Checks and Anomaly Detection:** The navigation software constantly monitors the consistency of position data. If the reported position jumps unrealistically, or if the signal characteristics (like signal strength or code phase) do not match what is expected based on the known position of the satellites, the system flags the data as potentially compromised.
3.  **Sensor Fusion:** Drones do not rely solely on GPS. They use Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs), air data sensors, barometers, and sometimes visual odometry (using cameras to track movement relative to the ground). If GPS data conflicts with data from these other sensors, the system can prioritize the trustworthy sensors or enter a safe-hold mode.
4.  **Geofencing and Expected Path Verification:** The drone's flight plan is pre-defined. The system knows exactly where the drone *should* be at any given second. If the device attempts to deviate significantly from this pre-approved path without a valid command from the ground control station, the onboard logic will typically refuse to execute the move or trigger an emergency landing in a safe location.
5.  **Encrypted Commands:** While GPS signals themselves are generally open and unencrypted (for civilian use), the commands sent from the ground station to the drone are heavily encrypted. An attacker might be able to feed false location data, but they cannot easily issue the "land here" or "release package" commands without the correct cryptographic keys.
