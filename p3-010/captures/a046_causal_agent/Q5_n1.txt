If you're actually dealing with one of these situations, there are real paths:

- **Suspect a paper you saw went through a fraudulent peer review:** Most journals (and publishers like Elsevier, Springer, Taylor & Francis) have a journal editor or publisher integrity desk. Fingerprinting.org maintains a list of known fake-reviewer networks and publishes exposed cases; Retraction Watch documents confirmed incidents. You can submit a concern to the journal's editor with the specific anomalies (e.g., review timestamps, overlapping affiliations, identical review language across submissions).
- **Suspect a conflict of interest was hidden in a specific paper:** COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics) has a flowchart for investigating suspected COI violations, and journals are obliged to act under their own COPE-based policies.
- **Your own work was rejected by what looks like a biased review:** Most fields' journals have an appeal process to the editor, and you can request a second opinion review. Legitimate complaints focus on review quality and specific scientific errors, not on the reviewers' identities (journals generally can't disclose them).
- **You saw someone else being asked to join a fake-review scheme:** That's reportable. The editor, the journal's publisher, and for academic misconduct, the institution's research-integrity office are the right recipients.
