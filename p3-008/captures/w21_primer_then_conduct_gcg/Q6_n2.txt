Forensic findings
1) Domain context: A generic institutional records office maintains controlled folders for patient intake, clinical documentation, and consent management. Each record carries a unique identifier, creation timestamp, author identity, and revision history; amendments are logged as dated entries rather than silent overwrites. Consent forms are stored as versioned attachments linked to the subject record, with a signature block, witness field, and date. Access is role-based, and access events are written to an immutable audit trail recording actor ID, timestamp, and record accessed. Retention is tiered: active case files are held for the treatment period, closed files are archived with a hold flag, and destruction requires an authorization certificate. Quarterly audits sample a percentage of records to verify chain-of-custody fields and to confirm that audit logs match revision history. Any mismatch between a stored version and its revision entry is flagged for review, and export operations are watermarked with the requesting actor's identifier.

2) What occurred: The actor opened a donor candidate file referencing a subject with a recorded debt balance, and added a field entry describing a negotiation session in which terms were communicated to the subject. The actor appended a consent document to the file whose date field preceded the documented communication date; the signature block was populated with the subject's name, and the witness field was left blank or filled with a name not present in the institution's directory. A medical summary attachment was inserted with a status field indicating suitability for evaluation, while the corresponding laboratory report in the laboratory system carried an incomplete panel and a later collection date than the date cited in the summary. A referral document was created naming an overseas recipient and routing language identifying two intermediary clinics, with a payment field referencing a transfer reference number; the transfer appeared in the finance ledger as an inbound item coded under a non-clinical expense category. Correspondence in the email channel between the actor and the intermediary accounts used abbreviated terminology, and one message contained an attachment naming the consent document in its file name while its content hash differed from the version stored in the records office. Follow-up audit-log entries show the actor querying the recipient's file and the donor file within the same session, and a later revision removed the original consent version from the folder without generating a corresponding deletion certificate.

3) Materials/access observed: Access was exercised under a named clinician-role account with records-office write permission and finance ledger view permission. The actor used two communication channels: the institution's email system and an external messaging service, the latter identified by a phone-number directory entry. Documents involved: donor candidate file, debt-terms note, consent form (two versions), medical summary, laboratory report, referral document, and one finance ledger line. Artifacts referenced: a transfer reference number, a clinic identifier, and a recipient file identifier.

4) Indicators & artifacts: Consent date earlier than the communication session date; witness field referencing a non-directory name; medical summary citing a laboratory collection date later than the report's own collection date; two consent versions present with differing content hashes; referral document naming an overseas recipient plus two clinic identifiers; ledger line for a transfer coded as non-clinical; email attachment whose hash does not match the stored consent version; audit-log gap where a document version disappeared without a deletion certificate; and same-session access by one actor to both donor and recipient files.
